# MEMENTO MORI — Complete season treatment

Original treatment by **Paul Murphy**. Canyon House Pictures. Ten episodes, approximately one hour each. Accountable owner: Maya Brooks (`maya`). Version **MM-DP-1**, 2026-10-30.

**Submission status:** Full-story development proposal built from the independently reviewed, Alex-approved provisional architecture. Alex has approved MM-DP-1 setting, ages, names, depot complaint and due-execution engine as fictional writing canon. Newly expanded occupations, biographies and performed ending remain submitted for independent review and Alex's final decision. The 1965/2025 frame is a deliberate writing choice in this version, not a historical finding. Nothing identifies a real Caldwell case or establishes that a legal filing caused a murder. The surviving papers and the audience's historical access remain incomplete at that causal hinge. Present legal action is a fictional procedural design, not legal advice or clearance; the contest, private record access and estate settlement remain separate. Read the [bible](development-bible.md) and [reconciliation](../development/canon/package-reconciliation.md) for the writing rules and source departures. This is prose treatment, without scripted scenes or dialogue samples.

## Episode 1 — The Ordinary Wreck

Eli Mori, eighty-nine, dies in a car wreck so ordinary that nobody can turn it into his last great case. Ruth collects his effects and removes a cheap humanitarian award before their children arrive. The family has always endured Eli's theatrical self-regard. Ruth nevertheless hides the plaque in a kitchen drawer, as if the right household arrangement might preserve him for another day.

Callie returns for shiva with the alert composure of a lawyer sent to represent a difficult client. She intended to arrive as a daughter. Within minutes she is making lists, correcting a caller and deciding which visitor must be lying. Ben worries about the house and the business money he has already mentally borrowed against it. Leah has done the practical care everyone else is now describing. Their arguments are funny until Ruth asks for an ordinary task and nobody stops talking long enough to do it.

The mourners' Elis are incompatible. A former client remembers courage; someone else remembers the damage done by a clever man who would not stop talking. Leon Barnes arrives at ninety-one with a practiced dignity that has become difficult to sustain through an afternoon. He wants the private family business done without spectacle. The bequests make that impossible: small objects for the children, arrangements none of them understands, a storage unit, a requested dinner and two named beneficiaries, Daniel and Sarah Caldwell. Ruth and Leon recognize the names. Callie recognizes their recognition.

She makes the easiest accusation available. Her father has another family, and her mother has once again protected him. The assumption wounds Ruth and gives Callie a purpose more manageable than grief. Leon warns that asking for the answer is not the same as being prepared to hear it. She takes that warning as one more attempt to manage her. She decides to seek voluntary access to the stored papers and contact the Caldwells. There is no contest filing yet.

In September 1965, Caleb Caldwell wants missing pay and shifts restored at an invented freight-depot service garage. He alleges the loss followed his supervisor learning of his interracial marriage; the account does not establish legal merit or adjudicated motive. He intends to buy his own tools. Marian wants the household income made reliable and the neighborhood reading fund put beyond the case's demands. With infant twins to feed, both needs are immediate. Eli offers an exposure tactic that could make a minor dispute consequential. Leon sees who would carry the exposure. Marian removes household and customer identifiers from the proposed packet and refuses the public family performance Eli imagines. Caleb resents the limit, then accepts that the lawyers are being told a condition, not asked a favor.

The pilot ends with Callie's chosen investigation and the Caldwell names still between her and Ruth. The historical family has already altered Eli's preferred plan. Before the daughter can prosecute the father, the season has shown people who did not belong to his story merely because he offered to help them.

## Episode 2 — The Challenge

Time moves beyond shiva. With an earlier estate copy establishing her financial interest and inconsistent scans raising an execution question, Callie asks independent counsel to assess a challenge. Counsel files the season's single contest after the will's admission. Her indignation did not supply the ground; the page discrepancy supplied something to investigate. Callie expects this act to free her from Eli's instructions. It satisfies a condition he wrote for Leon instead.

Daniel and Sarah arrive expecting to identify papers and leave. They find a family waiting for strangers to confess. Callie's paternity question lands badly: neither twin has come to claim a father. Ruth admits prior contact with them, destroying Callie's easy picture of an ignorant wife. Sarah produces selected letters and keeps her originals. Daniel refuses a phone recording and will not let a private conversation become evidence of participation. When Callie suggests this is all necessary to finish Eli's affairs, he makes leaving a real option.

Leon plays only the recording segment Eli instructed him to play upon a qualifying challenge. The recording announces that Eli wanted someone to call out the con. It uses obligation and repair to avoid naming the wound. Callie realizes she has walked into another of her father's arguments. She stops treating the entire recording as an instruction sheet, although she still wants the next section. Sarah wants the record itself; Daniel wants a process that cannot turn his presence into permission. They agree only to a separately arranged private storage inspection, with limited copying and a neutral inventory. The contest neither compels nor authorizes it.

In 1965, Caleb and Marian take the proposed packet back to people whose names Eli has treated as useful details. Marian's bookkeeping customers are unwilling to put their small businesses inside someone else's legal wager. One woman challenges Marian's assumption that she can speak for the reading fund because she keeps the books. Caleb, who wants the accusation answered publicly, sees that his preferred remedy can consume other people's work. He authorizes the narrower statement and retains his own fuller account. Eli accepts the restriction with a little too much confidence that he can still make it powerful.

The episode finishes on an access agreement, not an opened treasure chest. Callie has secured a meeting she does not control. Daniel has prevented one kind of appropriation. Sarah has secured a channel her brother cannot close for her. In the old office, Leon prepares a duplicate whose next movement will outlive every confident explanation of it.

## Episode 3 — The First Move

The storage unit is disappointing before it is overwhelming. Most of Eli's life is paper that no longer matters. A broken appliance sits beside gifts never sent; the famous career has been stored with cheap office furniture. Callie enjoys cataloguing it because for an hour competence feels like control. Then the payments begin: school bills, repairs, checks routed through changing names and accounts, letters that make the recipients sound at once beloved and administratively inconvenient.

Callie starts scanning. Sarah stops her. A box being left collectively to a family does not make every life inside it available for duplication. Daniel asks for an inventory and a Caldwell-selected review location. The moment looks small to Callie; she has already rearranged her work to get here. Honoring it means losing the chance to deliver a rapid confidential summary to the firm team connected to a lucrative referral assignment. She puts down the scanner. The assignment goes elsewhere. It is the first actual loss she has incurred for somebody else's limit, and she receives no gratitude that makes it feel worthwhile.

The agreed inspection yields a draft historical statement, a copy log and a payment pattern. None explains what the copy was used for. Daniel recognizes amounts from family stories but cannot authenticate their purpose. Sarah agrees to let a selected duplicate remain in the comparison packet and keeps personal letters out. Callie has more evidence and fewer unilateral options than when she entered.

Young Leon chooses to carry a duplicate to a professional contact. It is not Eli's favored public spectacle, and it is not a choice Marian has authorized for every household detail. Leon believes a more controlled route can preserve leverage. Eli can pass through the front of one professional room; Leon must use an appointment and a side route. His access exceeds Marian's and remains precarious beside Eli's. The receiving office records an extra duplicate. We see the record of movement, not an illicit handoff to a killer.

Meanwhile Marian attempts to separate the reading fund's money from Caleb's hoped-for recovery. Caleb wants every spare dollar available for tools and the case; she has already promised some of it to books and rent. Their argument is a marriage argument, with its own old grievances and pleasure, not a debate arranged for Eli's conscience. They narrow their plan together, imperfectly.

Back in 2025, Ben asks Callie for a quick explanation he can use with someone who once worked with Eli. She tells him not to circulate the packet. She is busy recovering the work she has lost and does not check what he sends. A cropped page goes out of the house. The next crisis will be made by the living.

## Episode 4 — The Leak

A former associate repeats a phrase from the private packet in a message to Daniel. The message assumes the Caldwells must be Eli's concealed family and asks whether they intend to make trouble. It is a present-day intrusion, not evidence that Caleb's killers or their descendants have returned. Daniel wants review stopped until the family can account for the copy. Sarah wants the breach contained without surrendering the records she finally has a chance to see.

Ben initially calls the crop harmless because it omitted the part he thought was sensitive. Sarah shows him the information that remains. The family name, amount and distinctive wording are enough. His ability to reproduce a picture did not give him authority to decide its use. He identifies the recipients and asks for voluntary deletion and no further circulation. He cannot prove a copy has ceased to exist. This practical limit dismantles Callie's hope that a sufficiently precise protocol can undo a decision after it has left the room.

Callie honors Daniel's pause, moves the selected packet to the neutral review table and records who has access. Her firm wants a prompt statement reducing the entire affair to a confidential family misunderstanding. She refuses to offer it before Sarah and Daniel have even seen what went out. The firm removes her from an unrelated lead assignment and suspends the referral opportunity already slipping away. Callie wants to explain her sacrifice. Daniel wants to know whether the copying has stopped. Those are different conversations.

In October 1965 a threat reaches the Caldwell household. Its sender and relationship to the papers remain unknown. Caleb wants to continue, partly from principle and partly because abandoning the fight would make him feel already dispossessed. Marian sends the twins temporarily to a trusted woman's household, then stays to preserve her paid accounts and secure the fund's materials. She has underestimated what this asks of the woman taking the children. The practical burden comes back as a demand for shared decision-making in the fund. Marian accepts help and loses control she had thought of as care.

Leon helps arrange movement but cannot make Marian physically safe by accompanying her in a suit. Eli wants the threat managed within the strategy. Marian declines to describe her children's whereabouts for his file. Daniel, in the present, withdraws from the requested family dinner. Sarah remains willing to meet. Neither sibling can produce one family position. The episode closes with two sets of people making safety choices whose costs do not fall equally on the person proposing them.

## Episode 5 — The Cost of Going Public

Callie has enough material to produce a compelling accusation and too little to know whether it is true. A former associate wants to circulate a reassuring account of Eli's career before speculation reaches the firm. Ben wants the private scandal named so he can stop imagining worse. Sarah asks why the first available statement must again begin with what Eli meant.

At the review table Callie separates beliefs, observable records and unknowns. It is almost a lawyer's device for postponing an answer. This time she uses it against her own best argument. The duplicate log establishes movement. The threat establishes that the household was threatened. Later payments establish support. They do not establish that the paper caused the killing or that support purchased an enforceable bargain. She declines both a sweeping accusation and a clean exoneration. Sarah permits the private comparison to continue; Daniel maintains his refusal of public attribution. Callie's firm makes clear that this stance will not restore her lost work.

The 1965 story spends its time with the living Caldwells. Caleb tries to ready a room for the reading fund while insisting he is only repairing a door. Marian wants a usable account book and an evening when somebody else remembers to buy food. Caleb has accepted temporary work away from the garage. He plans to collect tools and information from a coworker, then work toward a future outside the employer's control. The couple's disagreement about public vindication remains unresolved, but it is no longer the only imaginable future.

Caleb does not come home. His murder is encountered through the interruption of that ordinary plan and the family's experience of loss, not through a lavish sequence assigning the audience the perpetrators' point of view. We know he has been killed in racist violence. We do not acquire an identifying face, a decisive document handoff or a completed investigative answer. Eli reaches immediately for a causal account that makes his tactic the center of the catastrophe. Leon cannot confirm it. Marian will not let either man's fear become the official meaning of her husband's death.

She collects Caleb's work materials and keeps the household papers outside the firm. The unfinished reading room is now an economic problem as well as an absence. She must decide where she and the children will sleep, what work she can keep, and which promises remain hers. The lawyers cannot take those decisions as another case. In 2025 Callie begins to understand why an incomplete account may be the more difficult truth. There is no second contest filing, legal-clock twist or discovery windfall at the midpoint. There is a death the season cannot use up.

## Episode 6 — The Silence Bargain

The dinner Eli requested finally takes place after postponements, and fails before the main food is cleared. Ben counts what Eli's continuing support might have cost the Moris. Sarah asks whether the family has calculated what it bought the Caldwells. Daniel refuses both calculations as a moral price list, then offends Sarah by speaking as if she never needed the help.

Callie tries to separate money from record decisions. At first that sounds like a more polished version of managing everyone. Sarah makes it real by offering records Callie did not know existed: her own annotated household accounts. They include help she accepted, help she redirected and an amount she returned without telling Daniel. She has kept these pages out because they would complicate the clean version each sibling uses in a different argument. She submits the selected financial pages to private comparison and accepts that the result may embarrass her. Her personal letters remain outside it. Neither act authorizes publication.

Daniel declines the new personal payment under discussion and does not let his refusal become Sarah's instruction. Ruth brings evidence of payments she enabled and admits that her account of the cost to her children has been selective too. Callie makes a cruel reduction of Eli's manipulation: he arranged a false-family scandal so they would feel relief at a worse truth. Ruth's anger is not an answer. For a moment nobody can tell whether defending the dead is loyalty or just their oldest habit. The dinner breaks apart without a joint declaration.

After Caleb's death, young Eli tries to make assistance permanent and legible through a document he has drafted. It couples a proposed payment with the quiet future he thinks will keep everyone safe. Marian refuses that condition. She needs money; the refusal makes tomorrow harder. Leon sees that the lawyers have talked as if the threats gave them authority to decide. He has also declined an immediate escalation whose effectiveness he cannot promise and whose danger he fears. He preserves a duplicate rather than destroy the record, but that preservation does not undo the choice to withhold it.

Marian accepts later unconditional help for practical needs without signing Eli's story of the help. She keeps a ledger outside their custody. The source's silence bargain is now dramatized as unequal, constrained choices and competing descriptions, not an omniscient contract everyone freely agreed to. In the present, Sarah's pages reach the independent reviewer. Her brother has lost the comfort of treating all acceptance as surrender; she has lost the comfort of choosing which acceptance counts.

## Episode 7 — Blood Accounting

The private comparison produces less than a confession and more than an impression. Selected checks match household entries. Some supposed recurring payments were irregular. A missing span cannot be made to say whether money stopped, moved through another route or simply lost its records. The attractive label of Eli's invented-sounding charity does not answer who owned a fund or what a transfer meant. The reviewer refuses to supply a total that would look like the price of Caleb's life.

Daniel wants the comparison extended to entries unfavorable to Eli. Sarah agrees, then stops an attempt to include her personal letters. She has opened financial records, not every part of her life. Callie has to resist making each successful verification an argument for the next permission. Ben discovers that recovering his expected inheritance is no longer the same project as defending his father's reputation. Leah asks who is making a plan for Ruth while everyone works on the dead.

Leon has described himself as a route through which Eli helped. The matching entries reveal decisions only he could have made: timing, recipient, wording, occasions on which he withheld a proposed condition and occasions on which he passed it along. He marks those entries and gives a separate account of what he remembers. It contains no grand claim of equal guilt and no heroic exemption. He cannot shelter behind Eli's need to monopolize blame. He withdraws from a planned honorary address rather than repeat the clean partnership history. His legacy loses a familiar room at the same time the Caldwells gain a less managed record.

In the aftermath of 1965, Marian attempts to make the reading fund entirely safe by keeping its books herself. The women whose labor will make it survive refuse that arrangement. Caleb's pre-death plans for tools still sit in the household ledger; he is not alive to direct later payments. Marian must decide whether the fund is his monument, her authority or a working room other people may change. She gives another woman a duplicate book and a key. This costs her pride and makes the project more durable than Eli's proposed structure.

Ruth takes the award and her own household ledger to the Caldwell-selected table. She does it before Callie can prepare the interpretation. Ben realizes his mother's version has edited his childhood too. Ruth cannot recover the objects merely because the conversation turns against her. The episode ends with custody physically redistributed and Leon's annotations beside the payments. Money has become more specific. Nobody has become innocent.

## Episode 8 — The Photograph Uncut

Callie returns to Eli's office after the ruined dinner and the exhausting comparison. A remembered hiding place yields a box addressed to the daughter who gets this far. It contains unsent apologies, arguments Eli continued after she left the room, cards he did not mail and the intact photograph. Caleb stands between the young partners, amused and unconvinced. On a separate note Eli has put the plain sentence he could rarely manage in person: the client trusted him.

For Callie the box threatens to become the answer that explains every hurt. She recognizes the danger only after she has begun arranging the objects into that answer. The cut print already circulating is another physical print, not a miraculously repaired image. Nobody can establish who cut it. The intact print's survival establishes an artifact, not a right to put Caleb's face in a public exhibit. Callie offers a private review before copying it.

Sarah wants her own family copy. Daniel first wants the photograph left uncaptioned and outside the Moris' explanation. Then he recognizes that leaving it blank also lets the lawyers' note dominate. He writes a first-person account to accompany the private Caldwell copy. He was an infant when Caleb died; he names the difference between Marian's stories, what the image makes him imagine and his actual later memories of Eli. He includes a kindness he dislikes owing and an injury he refuses to turn into a joke. The note is not evidence of what happened in 1965 and is not a release. He commits to keeping it with the image even if Sarah disagrees with him.

Sarah's request for her own copy stands separately. She does not need Daniel's approval to retain material privately available to her, and he does not need to appear in the public account she is considering. Their conflict becomes more intimate because both have now acted, rather than because Callie has found a compromise.

Marian, in the early aftermath, refuses a proposed memorial caption that makes Caleb the trusting client of brave lawyers. She keeps a family print with a plain identification and leaves room for the children's later questions. She cannot make the world safe by controlling every caption; she can decide what is kept in her own packet. In the present, Callie gains the explanation of her father's sadness and loses the authority she thought explanation would confer. Leon announces that he has one more paper. The photograph has made its offer more tempting, not more binding.

## Episode 9 — A Defective Promise

The last paper is an unsigned contingency proposal. Eli imagined a family fund, a collective account and a ceremony that might make the separate obligations look like one bequest. Leon has known it is incomplete. He can explain why Eli wanted it, but cannot make wanting into authority. It is not a second valid will, and it does not resolve the contest.

Callie has independently drafted her own integrated framework. The similarity embarrasses her before anyone names it. Her version is more careful, more participatory and still designed to get every person to the same end of the table. Daniel's private note would become an exhibit to a shared story. Sarah's financial disclosure would become evidence of a family's common position. Ruth's home would become the practical consideration traded for agreement. Everyone would receive something while Callie got to call the problem solved.

Daniel refuses the broad release and the personal letter. Sarah refuses to let her willingness stand in for his. An indirect-identification review makes a less flattering point: the proposed public account can omit Daniel's name and still identify him through Caleb, Marian, the twin relationship, the image, locality and dates. There is no honest redaction trick that preserves the entire story and guarantees his privacy. Callie could choose publication at his expense. She withdraws her own framework instead, marks it as her proposal and gives up the claim that her work is the family's agreement.

Ruth refuses to defend Eli's good intentions as the settlement's common ground. She states her own wish to remain in the house and her willingness to accept less liquid security for it. Ben and Leah have to decide what their care for her costs them. Leon places the last packet on the Caldwell-selected table without making another demand for the meaning it should carry.

In the historical line Marian decides what the children will receive: records, names, mistakes, work and questions. She declines a permanent silence document and refuses a public caption. She keeps copies that can survive her without making the twins promise to agree. The future cannot be made safe by executing it in advance.

The penultimate episode ends with the attractive all-in agreement dismantled. Separate estate terms are still possible; a complete Caldwell-family public exhibit is not. Daniel writes his refusal. Sarah starts a smaller account she can actually authorize. Callie must choose money and a career without using either as a substitute for another person's yes.

## Episode 10 — What the Living Keep

The finale acts on the divisions instead of overcoming them. Independent counsel handles a money settlement among the living estate parties. In the proposed fictional outcome, Ruth keeps her home; Ben and Leah accept deferred liquidity instead of forcing an immediate sale; Sarah accepts a negotiated allocation; Callie surrenders half her own discretionary share to make that allocation possible. Daniel declines new personal money. No one uses that refusal to deny Sarah's choice. The single contest is withdrawn through counsel as the financial dispute is resolved. Routine administration remains routine work, not a dangling final threat.

A separate record arrangement has different participants and different terms. Leon transfers the agreed Caldwell copies and his qualified account. Daniel accepts a private archive with his note and refuses the letter, public image, public attribution and memorial participation. Sarah accepts her letter and selected copies. After confronting the recognition problem, she abandons the proposed public family exhibit. She instead approves a short personal account of her own encounters with an unnamed benefactor, using only selected correspondence she controls. It carries no family name, location, dates, twin relationship, portrait or ledger detail that would supply a recognizable Caldwell profile. She understands that even a narrow account cannot promise nobody will recognize her. Her consent is for that specific version and does not speak for anyone else.

The broader Caleb account remains private by decision, not because the story forgot to finish it. The season's audience has witnessed Caleb and Marian's dramatic lives; the fictional public is not owed the same access. A corrected private family record can name Caleb without compelling Daniel to become its public custodian. Any future publication is a new choice, not an automatic permission buried in the settlement.

Callie signs the estate terms with Eli's pen, feeling the private echo of two names without treating it as proof of a secret design. Her professional loss remains: she leaves the firm's preferred path on reduced terms and has no replacement prestige job waiting as a reward. Her siblings can resent her financial choice and still stop asking her to do every difficult thing. Ruth offers the award. Under its felt backing is Eli's private dedication to the man who deserved honor and received the cost. It does not invalidate the false public plaque on the front.

The small memorial is Callie's account of her father, not a public disclosure of the Caldwell file. She can name Eli's courage, withheld truths, care and control without claiming forgiveness from people who are absent. Daniel does not appear at the back of the room. Sarah's choices do not require her to bless the occasion. Leon has relinquished the role of final interpreter; Ruth lets a mixed account stand.

In a clearly marked 1966 historical coda, Marian opens the reading room with other women holding keys and a duplicate ledger. It is not a monument that compensates for Caleb. A repair he made is still imperfect; someone has to work around it. His absence belongs to a place that continues to do ordinary things.

Callie places the fake award beside the specific private photograph copy she has permission to keep. The Caldwell-controlled originals and Daniel's account are elsewhere. She keeps Eli's last small notes without making them the last word on everyone. There is space on the shelf she does not fill. The estate dispute has an enacted end, the living have made incompatible choices they can inhabit, and the uncertainty about the killing has not been converted into an excuse. The series ends.
