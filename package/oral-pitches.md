## Short pitch

*MEMENTO MORI* starts with a fake humanitarian award in a dead man's glove compartment.

The dead man is Eli Mori, a lawyer who could make a gift feel like a trap. His wife hides the award before the children arrive. His daughter catches her. Her father has managed to start the first argument at his own shiva without attending.

Callie Mori is a lawyer too, largely because her parents were very good at making their wishes feel like her choices. She arrives ready to organize a funeral, tolerate her siblings and get out. Then Eli's will gives his children objects and names two strangers as beneficiaries. Daniel and Sarah Caldwell. Ruth recognizes the names. So does Leon, Eli's old law partner. Nobody wants to tell Callie why.

She knows exactly what this is. Another family. Her father had secret children and left her to clean it up. For once she has an explanation simple enough to enjoy.

The show runs on two timelines. In the present, Callie presses against the estate, her mother and Leon's sealed records. In 1965 Birmingham, Caleb Caldwell and his wife Marian are building a life with newborn twins. Caleb wants to fight the harm done to him. Marian wants a voice in the risks the lawyers are prepared to take with their household. Young Eli wants to do something brave and be recognized for it. Leon understands that a clever strategy and a safe strategy are different things.

Caleb's story is the life happening before it becomes a file. There is work, ambition, marital disagreement, humor and a future people have every reason to want. At the season's midpoint he is killed. The sequence of danger, document movement and legal tactics does not hand us a clean causal verdict. What follows is a fight over how the survivors will live and what the lawyers may say on their behalf.

In the present, Daniel and Sarah are Caleb's children. The payments from Eli mattered to their lives. They disagree about what that means. Daniel refuses to become part of a dead lawyer's redemption. Sarah won't pretend help never happened, but she won't let gratitude stand in for permission either.

That's the engine across ten episodes. A legal challenge puts pressure on the estate. Access to the older record has to be negotiated separately. Every new object changes who can ask for what, who can refuse and what Callie will lose by honoring an answer she dislikes. Her family is good at turning a wound into an argument. The Caldwells are under no obligation to stay for it.

The ending doesn't deliver a better will that makes everything fair. It makes Eli's living children stop executing his idea of fairness. Daniel keeps a private account. Sarah chooses a narrower public one. Money cannot buy either choice. Callie gives up part of her inheritance and professional standing without earning the right to be forgiven.

Paul Murphy originated the treatment. The tonal starting points he gave us were *Succession* and *Mississippi Burning*: family wit and power colliding with danger that a lawyer cannot talk away. This is ten approximately hour-long episodes with a complete ending. The promise is a mystery in which understanding your father doesn't let you off the hook for what you do next.

## Longer pitch

The first object in *MEMENTO MORI* is a fake humanitarian award.

Eli Mori dies in an ordinary car wreck. There is no secret assassin to find. His wife Ruth finds the award among his things, understands how the children will read it and puts it out of sight. Callie catches her doing it. The mourning has barely begun and she already knows the family's assignment: make him look better.

Callie has spent a long time resisting that assignment. Eli was charming, evasive and exhausting. He could make any grievance sound like a misunderstanding of the question. When he couldn't answer, he made a joke. When he couldn't apologize, he gave an object. People outside the family still talk about the brave civil-rights lawyer he once was. Callie knows the father who trained her for a profession she did not choose and remained just out of reach when she needed anything he couldn't invoice, evade or wrap.

So the pilot brings her home to a house full of competing witnesses. A client remembers a rescue. A sibling remembers a debt. Ruth controls the traffic and the version of Eli each room is allowed to contain. Callie tries to do the competent thing: get through the mourning, identify the paperwork and keep her family from making a spectacle. Competence is one of the ways she avoids grief.

Then Leon arrives. He was Eli's partner, friend and witness across decades. He carries a particular authority in the family because he doesn't spend it as Eli did. He also carries instructions he is not ready to discuss.

The will gives the Mori children a strange assortment of objects. Money goes where they don't expect it. Two beneficiaries appear whom they have never heard of: Daniel and Sarah Caldwell. Their mother has heard of them. Leon has too. Callie sees the recognition before either person can hide it.

Her explanation is immediate. Eli had another family. He made her become a lawyer and now needs one last favor from beyond the grave. The explanation has the great advantage of confirming everything she already thinks about him.

Episode One ends with her choosing to investigate. She has not filed a contest. She has made the more personal decision to treat the estate as something she can expose. Episode Two gives that decision an outward form: a challenge to the will and an encounter with people who do not accept her terms. The challenge is an estate pressure. It does not magically give her the Caldwells' papers, open Leon's cabinets or make anyone consent to being interviewed.

That distinction is the series in miniature. Callie can pursue a remedy. She cannot make another family become the evidence for her understanding of her father.

The second timeline begins in 1965 Birmingham. Caleb Caldwell is a Black man with work, a temper, newborn twins and a future he intends to defend. His wife Marian is white. Her developed story gives her work and community commitments of her own. Their disagreement is intimate before it becomes strategic: what can this household risk, who gets to decide and what happens to the rest of their plans while the men are discussing the case?

Young Eli sees an opportunity to force the law to behave. He wants justice, and he wants to be the man who produces it. Both desires fit in the same room. Leon understands the city and the price of access differently. The two lawyers do not face the same exposure merely because they agree to work together. Their partnership is a source of energy and conflict, not proof that either has understood the limits of his power.

Marian refuses the wider exposure Eli would prefer. Caleb agrees to a narrower move. That means the historical scenes have an immediate question to play. We aren't cutting back to watch a clue being manufactured. We're following a family make decisions the audience knows will matter and cannot yet fully interpret.

In the present, Callie finds traces of years of support. Payments, letters, objects, a photograph with someone cut out. She wants to scan, compare, assemble and conclude. Daniel stops her. Sarah permits something narrower. Now a scene about a box becomes a scene about authority: whose room is this, whose hand is on the paper and who said it could leave?

Daniel is not withholding because the plot needs another hour. He has a coherent objective. He does not want Eli's money to purchase one more performance from his family. Sarah has another coherent objective. Help did affect their lives. Her resentment includes that fact. She doesn't owe Daniel a simpler feeling any more than she owes Callie an interview.

That split lets the present grow beyond Callie's investigation. Daniel can commission a private examination, write his own account and keep it private. Sarah can submit records that complicate her preferred explanation, hold back a personal letter or authorize a limited account without speaking for her brother. Their choices alter what Callie can do next.

Across the season, the episodes have different pressures. The opening is mourning and suspicion. The second is a challenge and a first encounter. A storage-unit access fight turns into custody. A threatening reference to a packet forces people to decide whether to halt, narrow or continue. A private accounting changes what can be said about the money without changing who gets to publish a photograph. A family dinner makes everyone confront the cost of Eli's generosity. An intact image changes an absence into a person who once stood there.

The historical line reaches Caleb's death at the midpoint. His death interrupts a life already in motion. The terror is not a surprise designed to make Eli interesting. Nor does editing a legal tactic beside a threat prove the tactic caused the killing. The show can make characters convinced of a connection while leaving them unable to demonstrate every step.

Afterward, Marian is still making decisions. She rejects the lawyers' preferred account, controls papers they would like to organize and refuses to let support settle what can be said. The past does not stop when Caleb does. That matters because the present is living inside the consequences of those choices as well as Eli's.

Ruth's story is also active. She knew about the support. She made a marriage around knowledge she did not share with her children. There are costs in stopping it, costs in allowing it and costs in turning those costs into an excuse. As the family aligns and realigns, she has to act before Callie is willing to understand her. She cannot remain the keeper of a secret until someone asks the perfect question.

Leon loses the protection of being only the dignified custodian. He identifies the records he moved and the choices he made. His responsibility is real. It is not a copy of Eli's. Present Leon is old, with a body the scene has to respect; he can still decide what he will surrender, refuse and contradict. His account complicates the record rather than authorizing the final explanation.

Callie's change is measurable. Early on she believes access will let her make a true account and a true account will justify the means. Respecting a Caldwell boundary costs professional advantage. Later she proposes a framework meant to solve inheritance and record questions together. It is intelligent, comprehensive and recognizably her father's child. Daniel and Sarah make its problem visible: one agreement can turn their different positions into her coherent conclusion.

She withdraws it. The loss is more than paperwork. She gives up the pleasure of being the person who finally solved this family.

In the finale, the estate and the record have different outcomes. A draft of Eli's wishes may show how he imagined peace. It cannot make peace binding on anyone. The living negotiate what they can actually agree to. Daniel refuses a public identity and keeps a private archive. Sarah permits a narrower account. Those decisions must coexist; one sibling does not become the signature for the other.

Callie gives up part of her inheritance and accepts continuing professional damage. That cost does not make her the moral owner of Caleb's story. It gives her a harder, smaller job: keep an honest record, honor the limits she accepted and live with a father who remains capable of care and harm in the same account.

The fake award and the restored photograph can finally occupy the same space. Neither cancels the other. Eli does not get an innocent ending. Callie does not get a clean explanation she can use as an inheritance. The family gets a record it no longer has the power to make unanimous.

Paul Murphy created the original treatment and its collision of family wit, mementos and a buried civil-rights history. *Succession* and *Mississippi Burning* are his tonal starting points, not a promise of another show's audience or scale. Our working format is ten approximately hour-long episodes with a complete ending. The historical case and expanded design are fictional. The development package makes no claim of human consultation, clearance, attachments or buyer interest.

The reason to watch is simple. Callie thinks she knows the kind of liar her father was. Finding out she is wrong doesn't turn him into a better father. It makes her decide what kind of lawyer, daughter and keeper of other people's stories she will be.

## Discussion prompts and grounded responses

**Why ten episodes?** Three interacting stories carry the length: an estate pressure that puts Callie's interests at risk; a present conflict over access, accounting and publication; and an active historical marriage/community/legal story. Each episode must change an option or a relationship. The full [season treatment](season-treatment.md), once reviewed and approved, carries the complete proof of that design.

**Is this a true story?** The Caldwell case and principal story are invented/composite. The preserved treatment is creative source material. Approved fictional choices are not historical findings, and researched claims retain their own evidence limits.

**Does the will contest force the confession?** Eli's fictional instructions may make filing relevant to what he wanted Leon to show. The filing itself does not create access, custody, disclosure or publication rights. Leon and the living families still make consequential choices.

**Does Callie solve Caleb's murder?** The season resolves its chosen family and record conflicts without promising a complete, verified identification of perpetrators or causal chain. The cost of an incomplete account is part of the ending.

**Who supplies the historical viewpoint?** Caleb, Marian, Eli and Leon pursue immediate objectives in scenes. The historical line is not simply whatever Eli says happened. The audience can see a choice while remaining uncertain about later attribution.

**What is Paul's personal connection?** Paul originated the treatment. No further personal history has been supplied for publication, and the package does not invent one. The [project statement](creator-statement-and-bio.md) describes the work in third person.

**Who is attached, and what does it cost?** No cast, producer, buyer or financier attachment is claimed. The [production overview](positioning-and-production.md) identifies concrete drivers for assessment; there is no priced budget or shooting schedule.
