## The show

Eli Mori survives a lifetime of dangerous arguments and dies in an ordinary car wreck. His eldest daughter Callie comes home prepared to bury a difficult father. Instead she finds an inheritance that looks like a badly disguised affair: insulting objects for his children, convoluted gifts to strangers, and two beneficiaries whose names make her mother and Eli's old partner stop talking. Callie is a lawyer because her parents wanted one. For once she plans to use that training against the man who gave it to her.

The strangers are Daniel and Sarah Caldwell, the sixty-year-old twins of Caleb Caldwell, a Black client killed in Birmingham when Eli and his partner Leon were young. They have spent their lives with the consequences of a story the Moris did not have to know. Eli's support helped, offended, relieved and controlled them in different measures. His will now asks them to perform his last confession in front of his children. Daniel refuses. Sarah wants the records before she decides what to refuse. Neither accepts Callie as a neutral investigator simply because she is angry with her father.

Across ten approximately hour-long episodes, the estate dispute becomes a contest over authorship. Callie can explain Eli, win an argument, collect the money and call herself brave—or she can stop making other people's lives useful to her explanation. In 1965, Caleb and his wife Marian try to build an ordinary future while two ambitious lawyers struggle over how much exposure a legal tactic can bear. In 2025, their children decide what survives, what can be shared and what remains private. The season finishes its estate story and its family story. It leaves historical uncertainty honestly unresolved rather than holding a killer's identity for another season.

The audience promise is intimate suspense: every episode changes an available action. A photograph becomes harder to use when its subject has a family; an accounting becomes less comforting when the recipient opens her own books; an inheritance becomes smaller when Callie stops using silence as leverage. The pleasures are family wit, material clues, sharp reversals, two generations of adversarial love, and the recognition that being correct can be another form of getting one's way.

## Format, movement and tonal grammar

This is a ten-part limited-series design with a complete ending. Approximate hour length is an editorial target, not a promise of exact running time. Episodes 1–5 braid the estate's opening with an independently active 1965 story. Episodes 6–10 continue the historical aftermath through Marian's work and choices; Caleb does not return alive after the midpoint except when an explicitly earlier moment is identified. The historical line does not become a slideshow of Eli's remorse.

The present can be crowded, funny and fast. Ben can sell an expensive theory about a trust while failing to fix the coffee machine; Leah can finish a caregiving task while everyone else explains how much they cared. Humor comes from these people competing for competence and affection. It never serves as the release valve immediately after Caleb's killing. The historical family has its own pleasure and humor: Caleb repairs things too thoroughly, Marian wants exact totals but rounds away her own indulgences, Leon knows when Eli's expensive confidence is about to become someone else's problem. Black life is present before danger and persists after loss.

Shiva is a particular family's gathering, not a universal display of Jewish observance. Ruth wants the house open and the ordinary work of feeding visitors done. Callie wants a private room and a precise account. Ben wants people to keep coming because an empty house is frightening. Leah notices which mourner washes a dish. Their practices vary without making one the official representative of Jewish life. The later estate meetings and memorial occur after shiva; the series does not stretch seven days of mourning into its entire present chronology.

Objects carry action rather than cryptographic destiny. An object can be sentimental, ridiculous, expensive to keep, or useful as evidence, and those uses can conflict. No object proves Eli was good. The final shelf holds the fake award beside a Caldwell-approved private copy of Caleb's photograph, with space between them. Callie has permission to keep that specific copy in her private room. The shelf is not a public exhibit or the solution to ownership of the original.

## Two timelines, three engines

**The estate:** Callie is personally interested, not counsel to the whole family. Independent counsel carries the contest. A prior estate copy gives her a concrete financial interest, and inconsistent attestation-page scans justify investigating execution. In the proposed Episode 2 action, counsel brings a good-faith due-execution challenge after admission. Eccentric bequests and anger are motives to investigate, not the legal ground. Later comparison cannot establish a clean invalidity case. The parties ultimately make their own negotiated allocation; nobody wins a surprise second will.

**The record:** Access to the storage room, permission to inspect, permission to copy, authenticity of a copy and permission to publish are different acts. Eli's recording condition is his own instruction to Leon. The contest does not create disclosure, freeze assets or deliver documents by magic. People voluntarily negotiate review and retain independent advice. The family can reach money terms while failing to agree on a public account; that separation generates the finale.

**The historical household:** Caleb wants pay and standing restored after an invented freight-depot service garage cuts his shifts and withholds earnings; he alleges the change followed his supervisor learning of his interracial marriage, without adjudicated proof of motive. He wants to use recovered wages to buy his own tools and stop depending on the same employer. Marian wants paid bookkeeping work and a small reading fund run with local women, not a ceremonial charitable project named by the lawyers. Their interests overlap but do not merge. Caleb wants his name restored in public; Marian refuses to put the twins, her customers and the reading-fund households into the pressure packet. She can be prudent and proud, generous and controlling. Her first narrowing choice alters the tactic before the lawyers can call it theirs.

The fictional case is not attached to an actual employer, victim or historical prosecution. A draft statement and copy trace survive. The series shows Eli's appetite for public pressure, Leon's delivery choice, the family's response to a threat and Caleb's death. It never shows a recipient taking the paper to the killers, identifies the killers, or retrospectively proves a filing caused the murder. A character's causal certainty is part of the drama. The audience knows some private choices the 2025 family cannot establish, but is denied the decisive causal link too.

## The relationships

### Callie Mori — 53 in 2025

Callie is an accomplished lawyer whose competence has become a way to postpone choosing her life. She can organize a catastrophe faster than she can say she is hurt. She wants proof that her father preferred another family; the simple accusation would let her keep the contempt that has protected her. Her flaw is not curiosity. It is treating access as entitlement, then mistaking the cleanest available explanation for consent.

Her parents' professional coercion is visible in an old law-school enrollment packet Eli completed before she decided, Ruth's precise memory of the practical career she rejected, and Callie's present habit of doing the family's work without asking why she volunteered. The private Callie/Caleb association can unsettle her, but a similar name does not prove she was manufactured as his replacement. Her parents shaped her toward defense and management; that harm is sufficient without a naming conspiracy.

Callie's voice is compressed, exact and funny when she wants to end a subject. In early episodes she turns a person's answer into her next question. Later she can leave an answer unharvested. Her first costly choice is to stop copying when Sarah says stop, sacrificing a lucrative referral assignment she could have retained by turning the records over to the firm's preferred team. Her firm then removes her from an unrelated lead assignment when she refuses its reputational containment plan. She finally chooses an exit on unfavorable terms rather than pretend she is an impartial steward the firm can advertise. This is a specific career setback, not disbarment or an inferred disciplinary finding.

At the ending she gives up half of her own negotiated discretionary share to improve Sarah's independent position, while retaining enough to live. She cannot buy Daniel's participation with it. Ben and Leah do not automatically admire the decision. Callie remains capable of being difficult. She has stopped using difficulty as proof that other people owe her an explanation.

### Daniel Caldwell — 60 in 2025

Daniel is a maintenance supervisor approaching retirement who knows the value of a system with an off switch. He wants the family papers under dependable private care and wants his own life to remain outside Eli's memorial. He is financially steadier than Sarah, which makes refusal easier for him and his advice less neutral than he thinks. His flaw is allowing a principled refusal to harden into a rule for his sister.

Daniel remembers Marian and his later encounters with Eli. He cannot remember Caleb, who died when the twins were infants. In Episode 8 he writes the distinction himself: what his mother told him, what the photograph makes him imagine, and what he actually witnessed when Eli came into their lives. His account includes a practical kindness by Eli he would rather omit, and an insult he will not soften. This is authorship at a cost, not a grudging concession to Callie. He keeps that account private beside the family photograph.

His voice is economical; he asks who will keep a copy before asking what it means. He can be dry, tender with tools and unexpectedly impatient with Sarah. He refuses Eli's final personal letter and any new personal estate payment. He accepts copies of the Caldwell file. He does not attend the public memorial and does not become a secret approving spectator. The people he protects are allowed to live without being his argument. His refusal limits what the finale can show to the public.

### Sarah Caldwell — 60 in 2025

Sarah has spent years doing freelance accounts and caring for Marian during her final illness. Her interrupted work, smaller savings and familiarity with Eli's practical help give her a different relationship to the money. She wants to see the whole record and eventually to make a narrow account possible. Her flaw is selective accounting: she wants everyone to recognize the help while initially shielding the financial choices that made that help complicated.

In Episode 6 she submits her own annotated household records for a private comparison, including a payment she redirected from a course deposit to urgent household costs and a return she never told Daniel about. Those entries do not make her dishonest about her father's loss. They make her a participant who can change what the financial record says. She risks Daniel's judgment and the loss of a simpler family account.

Her voice tests a claim by giving it a practical example. She can be warm without being easy. She accepts Eli's letter for herself and Sarah's negotiated estate allocation without forgiving him. She approves a short account of her own encounters with an unnamed benefactor and selected copies of her own correspondence. She refuses the proposed Caldwell-family public exhibit after seeing that even redacted genealogy would identify Daniel. No one calls the smaller account a public restoration of Caleb's entire history. That larger publication does not happen this season. Sarah's choice remains active because she chooses something narrower after she has enough information to judge it.

### Caleb Caldwell — 28 in 1965

Caleb is a mechanic with a worker's exact knowledge of what a bad repair can cost. His goal is a job and a future in which his own tools are his to keep. The withheld wages and lost shifts threaten both. He enjoys competition, takes unflattering photographs of repaired machines and can make Marian laugh by pretending a household inconvenience is a major engineering project. He dislikes being told he has courage when he is trying to obtain pay.

He wants a public correction and initially takes Eli's willingness to go into a hostile room as proof that the room can be made to answer. His flaw is confusing the shame of retreat with its practical consequence. He can hear Marian's limit as doubt in him rather than a different exposure she has to carry. He ultimately accepts the narrower draft but keeps the original account in his own words. Leon and Eli are not the only men who may decide how plainly he speaks.

In Episode 5 Caleb makes a choice about living: he agrees to take temporary work away from the garage, finish the room for the reading fund and continue the fight without letting it consume the household. He leaves to collect tools and earnings information from a coworker. His murder interrupts that practical plan. It is neither the fulfillment of a noble death wish nor a lesson he gives Eli. We do not stage perpetrators as a mystery-game prize. His absence changes the household's work, money, laughter and daily movement before it explains any lawyer.

### Marian Bell Caldwell — 25 in 1965; dies in 2012 at 72

The preserved treatment identifies Caleb's wife as an unnamed white woman. Alex has approved Marian's development name, paid work and reading-room objective; the fuller characterization below remains part of this story submission, not a recovered biography. Marian has paid bookkeeping customers, a taste for dance records she does not budget for, and a reading-fund ambition that is partly generosity and partly a wish to be indispensable. Her community connections are individual relationships, not a claim that interracial marriage brings uncomplicated institutional acceptance in this setting.

She can be impatient with a slow reader and ashamed afterward. She underestimates how her insistence on keeping work and papers in Birmingham will burden the trusted woman who temporarily takes the twins. She corrects that mistake by surrendering control of part of the reading fund to the other women. Her goal survives her husband's death without becoming an inspirational consolation for it.

She narrows Eli's proposed packet; sends the twins temporarily into a trusted household while she resolves wages and work; refuses a payment coupled to Eli's preferred silence; accepts later unconditional practical support; gives another woman a duplicate ledger; refuses an Eli-authored caption for Caleb's photograph; and establishes a private family packet in which she names both help and harm. Her post-death danger constrains her choices. It does not make the lawyers the authors of her consent.

Her voice is specific about quantities, unreliable about her own tiredness, and capable of a sharp joke with Caleb. After he dies, the change is a missing partner in the room, not the disappearance of her whole personality. Her 2012 death is a family-history fact in this proposed fiction. It does not invite an invented consultation or a real-person identification.

### Eli Mori — 29 in 1965; dies at 89 in 2025

Eli wants the law to make him consequential. In 1965 he has bravery and access as well as vanity. He can enter spaces that refuse Leon and return pleased with his own courage while Leon manages the cost. He proposes the pressure tactic; later he participates in withholding a record. He is not a murderer and is not exonerated because he intended a good outcome.

Money is his way of continuing a relationship without bearing an answer. He can help with a school bill and turn that help into a theory of responsibility the recipient did not choose. The recording, object bequests and hidden box extend that habit after death. His voice moves from joke to qualification at the moment someone asks for a plain answer. He sometimes gets the plain answer right and then cannot bear to send it.

The fake humanitarian award has two inscriptions, public vanity and private accusation. Neither is the real Eli that cancels the other. His last papers include an unsigned contingency plan for a family fund. Its emotional attraction is strong precisely because it would make everyone stop fighting. It has no resolving authority. Eli remains funny, brave, cowardly, caring and controlling. A complete account of him does not restore Caleb's life.

### Leon Barnes — 31 in 1965; 91 in 2025

The source uses Leon and the firm sign supplies Barnes. Do not expand him to Leonard. Young Leon wants a practice that can keep its clients alive and its doors open. He knows the limits of Eli's access and sometimes uses that knowledge to justify withholding an argument from the client. In the historical packet movement he chooses to deliver a duplicate to a professional contact rather than relinquish the case to Eli's public tactic. His copy trail survives; who subsequently saw it does not.

Leon faces racist exclusion and bodily danger Eli does not face on equal terms. He also has professional literacy, contacts and custody that Marian lacks. Neither difference erases the other. After Caleb's death he declines an immediate, uncertain escalation he fears will endanger the household, while preserving a copy outside Eli's working file. This is a constrained choice he must own, not a symmetrical second vote for Eli's sin.

At 91, Leon's pace, fatigue and need for assistance are visible; his age is not decorative. He wants his professional legacy to include the people he helped without letting the Caldwell matter disappear into Eli's legend. He first calls himself a courier. When the audit reaches entries he routed, he marks them in his own hand and states what he cannot remember. Later he removes himself from a planned honorary address rather than deliver the clean partnership story again. That visible loss of standing accompanies his surrender of sole custody. His voice is deliberate and often punctures Eli's theatrics; deliberate speech does not make him an oracle.

### Ruth Mori — 85 in 2025; 25 in 1965

Ruth wants her home to remain a home after Eli's death rather than an evidence room or a property her children liquidate. She is observant about people and occasionally ruthless about the version of them that will survive a difficult day. Her flaw is calling containment care long after the immediate danger has passed. She knew about the Caldwell support early, enabled transfers and resented what Eli's unspoken debt took from her family.

Ruth's independent present action comes before the ending: she takes the award and her household payment ledger to the Caldwell-selected review table without Callie's permission. Ben then sees how much of the Mori-family sacrifice story came from Ruth's selective accounts. Ruth loses her authority as the one person who could explain Eli if she chose. She can also correct Callie's claim that every marriage choice was made for the children. In the finale she elects to remain in the house with less liquid security rather than let Callie purchase peace by selling it over her head. Her final gift of the award is an offer Callie may refuse.

### Ben Mori and Leah Mori

Ben, 49, runs an inconsistent event-photography business. He wants his share liquid and the family back on speaking terms, in that order when frightened. He uses jokes to solicit rescue and can make an ugly but accurate point about Callie's ability to afford a principle. He circulates a crop from the will packet to a former associate seeking an explanation, creating the present-day breach. He stops pretending it was harmless when Sarah sees it. His repair is to identify the recipients and retrieve voluntary copies; he cannot promise an email can be made never to have existed.

Leah, 45, is a physical therapist whose work and years of nearby caregiving make her suspicious of grand arrivals. She wants recognition of actual care and a workable plan for Ruth. Her flaw is using competent exhaustion to refuse any new information. She takes custody of the ordinary bequests when the investigation turns them into props, then chooses one without accepting Eli's interpretation of it. She supports Ruth remaining in the house even though it delays her own proceeds. Neither sibling becomes an interchangeable chorus or is required to approve Callie's ending.

## Fictional chronology and age arithmetic

MM-DP-1 selects 1965 Birmingham and a September–November 2025 Alabama present as a writing frame. This is an affirmative fiction choice; the old 1963 alternative remains in the earlier architecture record but is not the simultaneous date of this version. All listed birthdays precede September except the twins' August birthday, which also precedes the present action. Ages are completed-year arithmetic, not inferred from the pen.

Eli and Ruth marry in 1963, at 27 and 23. They are 36 and 32 when Callie is born. The firm begins in 1964; Caleb is its first substantial contested matter the next year, not proof that neither lawyer had ever served a client. Historical Episodes 1–5 run from September to late October 1965; Episodes 6–10 cover the first weeks of aftermath and a clearly marked 1966 coda. Present Episode 1 begins September 22, 2025; the final personal memorial and distribution decisions occur in November. Intervening legal administration is compressed through dated correspondence and ordinary time passage, not one invented statutory clock.

## The season's ten movements

1. **The Ordinary Wreck:** Grief becomes suspicion; Marian narrows the lawyers' appetite for exposure. Callie chooses voluntary access and contact with the named strangers, with no contest filing yet.
2. **The Challenge:** A single, independently advised contest and Eli's chosen recording condition bring the Caldwells into a room they can leave. Daniel refuses recording; Sarah opens a private channel.
3. **The First Move:** Storage access produces a copying conflict and Callie's first lost opportunity. Leon's historical duplicate leaves a trace without explaining the later violence.
4. **The Leak:** Ben's present breach splits Daniel and Sarah's response. Marian's temporary household separation changes her work, marriage and support network.
5. **The Cost of Going Public:** Callie refuses a premature exonerating account. Caleb's interrupted plans and murder create an irreversible loss; Marian keeps the family papers.
6. **The Silence Bargain:** Sarah risks her own financial record and the delayed dinner breaks apart. Marian refuses Eli's condition and accepts the risk of being harder to help.
7. **Blood Accounting:** Comparison establishes selected transfers and material gaps. Leon's own annotations displace his courier defense; Marian gives away control of part of the fund so it can survive.
8. **The Photograph Uncut:** Callie's hidden box is no longer the final authority. Daniel writes an inherited-memory account and keeps it private. Ruth relinquishes control of the award and ledger.
9. **A Defective Promise:** The unsigned plan tempts everyone with a frictionless solution. Callie withdraws her own unified framework and accepts divided outcomes.
10. **What the Living Keep:** Separate estate and record choices are carried out. Daniel keeps privacy; Sarah chooses limited personal disclosure; Callie pays in work and money. The historical coda belongs to Marian's living project.

## Props, knowledge and the endpoint

The [custody and consent register](../development/canon/custody-consent.csv) assigns distinct jobs. The award stages reputation; the pen links paperwork to habit without authenticating a year; the two photographic prints stage representation; ledgers compare material help; the video exposes posthumous manipulation; the unsigned plan exposes its temptation. The watch, chess clock and menorah remain personal bequests rather than mandatory additional clue boxes. No one has to decode an object to deserve it.

Daniel receives a private family archive and keeps his note there. Sarah receives her own authorized copies and decides what to do with her letter. The public Caldwell exhibit is abandoned because the proposed redactions cannot protect Daniel against indirect identification. A much narrower account of Sarah's own encounters uses no family name, photograph, locality, dates, twin relationship or employment identifiers; even this carries an acknowledged residual recognition risk, which she understands. Only material she personally controls is used. Nothing in her consent speaks for Daniel, Marian, Caleb or the image's creator. A later broader publication would require a new decision and any appropriate rights process; it is not secretly authorized by the ending.

Callie and the other living estate parties execute separately negotiated terms in the fictional finale. Ruth remains in her home; Ben and Leah accept delayed liquidity; Sarah accepts an allocation that includes half of Callie's negotiated discretionary share; Daniel refuses new personal money. The contest is withdrawn through counsel as part of resolving the money dispute, not because a draft will proves anything. Ordinary administration continues without remaining dramatic leverage. No record permission is purchased or conditional on that settlement.

Callie's memorial names her father's actual contradictory actions without narrating the private Caldwell material. Daniel is absent by choice; Sarah has no obligation to attend or bless it. Leon transfers the agreed packet and marks its limits. Ruth stops correcting everyone toward a good-man verdict. In the historical coda, Marian opens the reading room with other women holding keys and a ledger she does not solely control. The project is small, imperfect and alive. In the present, Callie places the award beside the private photograph copy and does not fill the remaining space. The season ends with choices enacted, not a cliffhanger about a missing page.

## Production-facing design limits

The present has five main repeat locations: Mori house/office, storage unit, modest Caldwell living/work spaces, neutral review room and Callie's workplace. Historical reuse centers on the small law office, Caldwell home/work area, garage and reading room. A limited exterior vocabulary establishes Birmingham without promising landmark access. The ordinary car wreck can be told through aftermath. Caleb's murder is not a set-piece chase or a spectacle; an interrupted arrival, absence and the household's response carry the event.

Eli and Leon need young and old casting; Ruth's 1965 presence is tightly bounded; Marian and Caleb anchor the historical ensemble. Period babies must remain babies. The present twins are sixty. Callie can mistakenly infer children from an earlier life because Eli was twenty-nine when they were born; this is possible arithmetic, not evidence of paternity. Do not stage them as young adults or pretend age alone disproves the theory. Props require two physical photo prints, an intact image with a distinct custody trail and a cut circulating print. Designs for historical papers remain fictional and must not reproduce real victim records as if they were Caldwell evidence.

No budget, schedule, buyer interest, attachments, professional legal clearance or external cultural consultation is claimed. Historical and legal research are advisory to the creative gates. Actual legal/rights and production assessment remain separate from approving this invented story. This bible adds no new factual claims about 1965 law or current probate; the [creative brief](../development/brief/creative-brief.md) and existing research registers retain their explicit limits. Paul Murphy remains the creator of the original treatment and retains his rights; agent ownership here means responsibility for documents.
