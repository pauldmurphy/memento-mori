# **MEMENTO MORI**

## *A 10 Episode Prestige Television Series*

By Paul Murphy

**GENRE:** Whydunit  
**TONE:** *Succession* meets *Mississippi Burning*. A sharp, cynical, dialogue-driven family prestige dramedy that collides head-on with a bleak, grounded, historical racial drama.

# **LOGLINE**

When Eli Mori, a once-respected civil rights attorney turned slippery family shyster, dies and leaves behind a baffling will that names two unknown heirs, his estranged daughter investigates why his estate looks like one final con; only to uncover the buried first case in 1960s Birmingham that broke him, cost a man his life, and left Eli spending the rest of his years disguising complicity and crushing guilt as love.

# **WORKING STORY IDEA**

Eli Mori dies in a stupid, ordinary car wreck.  
No conspiracy. No murder. No grand last stand. Just a wrecked car, a dead old lawyer, and a cheap fake award found in his glove compartment.  
The plaque reads:  
**HUMANITARIAN OF THE YEAR** **ELI MORI**  
It is ugly. Badly engraved. Wrong font. Wrong date. The issuing organization appears not to exist. It looks like exactly the kind of thing Eli would have carried: a fraudulent tribute to himself, manufactured by a man who spent his life turning paper into performance.  
His widow, Ruth, retrieves the plaque before their children see it. That is the first lie after Eli's death. But Eli's family has been living inside his lies for decades.  
**CALLIE MORI**, Eli's eldest daughter, arrives at the family home for shiva carrying years of contempt disguised as composure. She almost hates being a lawyer; Eli and **RUTH** forced the profession on her the way good Jewish parents do, dragging her into a career she never truly wanted. She knows the Eli Mori everyone else met: charming, theatrical, funny, brilliant in small bursts, impossible to pin down, always ready with a story, a joke, a loophole, a lie. He had once been a respected civil rights attorney, or so everyone said. In his youth he was brave. In old age he became something smaller: an emotional fog machine in a cheap suit. A lawyer who talked around the truth so long he can look like he had forgotten where he had buried it.  
To Callie, Eli was not complicated. He was evasive. He loved in gestures and hid in jokes. He promised without committing. He apologized without naming the wound. He gave gifts that felt like traps. He turned every family argument into a cross-examination and every vulnerable moment into a bit. By the end of his life, Eli Mori was a shitty father, a slippery lawyer, a sentimental coward, and a man who could make even kindness feel like a scam.  
Callie catches Ruth hiding the fake award in a kitchen drawer. Ruth says, too quickly: "Your father was a good man." Callie looks at the plaque: "Good men don't buy their own awards." Ruth does not answer.  
The house begins to fill with mourners. Relatives. Old clients. Former secretaries. Neighbors. Synagogue people. Lawyers who admired Eli once and avoided him later. Men and women who remember different versions of him. One mourner says Eli saved her brother from prison. Another says Eli ruined a case by getting too clever. A former client calls him a hero. Another calls him a crook with better vocabulary. A judge's widow says Eli was the bravest young attorney she ever saw. A man from an old civil rights nonprofit leaves without signing the guest book. Every story makes Eli less stable. He was not merely loved or hated. He was interpreted.  
Then **LEON** arrives. Leon is old now, dignified, contained, and carrying a silence so old it has become part of his posture. He was Eli's law partner, co-founder, witness, accomplice, rival, and friend. A Black attorney who built a firm with Eli in Birmingham in the 1960s, when the idea itself was an insult to the city.  
Callie knows the family mythology: Eli and Leon, the impossible firm. Two young lawyers standing together when it mattered. The photographs are still in boxes somewhere. Eli with wild hair and righteous eyes. Leon in a dark suit, calm even then, looking like he already knew the price of every room he entered. Callie never fully believed the legend. Not because the danger was false. Because Eli had spent too many years using the legend as moral collateral.  
Leon greets Ruth with tenderness and Callie with caution. He has come to read the will. The will reading is supposed to be private. It becomes a detonation. Eli's will is not merely eccentric. It is insulting.  
His children receive objects instead of meaningful assets: a watch, a fountain pen, an old case file, a broken chess clock, a fake award, a photograph with someone cut out of it, a menorah nobody remembers owning. The estate is routed through strange trusts. Money is assigned to a fake-sounding charity. A storage unit is left to the family "collectively, but not cooperatively." There is a mandatory dinner after shiva. There are sealed instructions Leon may not open unless the family formally challenges the will.  
And there are two unknown beneficiaries. Twins. **DANIEL** and **SARAH CALDWELL**. No one in the family knows who they are. Except Ruth. Callie sees it immediately. The way Ruth looks down. The way Leon stops breathing. The way the name lands in the room like something old and already dead.  
Callie assumes the obvious: Eli had another family. Of course he did. Of course the emotional coward with the fake award and the half-truths and the unexplained absences had secret children somewhere. Of course Ruth knew. Of course Leon covered it. Of course the will is Eli's final little burlesque, forcing his legitimate family to discover his illegitimate one while everyone is still sitting shiva.  
Callie decides to challenge the will. Leon warns her not to. Not angrily. Not legally. Almost sadly. Callie tells him she is done being managed by dead men and their living attorneys. The challenge is filed.  
Shiva becomes an investigation. Callie turns the dining room into a war room. Condolence cards sit beside legal pads. Old photographs are pinned to corkboard. Eli's sentimental bequests are catalogued like evidence. Ruth watches her daughter convert mourning into prosecution and says nothing. The family wants the scandal contained. Callie wants it named.  
She follows the clues. The fake award leads to no banquet, no ceremony, no organization. It is self-invented glory. The storage unit contains boxes of old civil rights case materials, birthday cards never sent, duplicate children's gifts, canceled checks, ledgers, old newspaper clippings, and decades of payments to the same family. The fake charity is real only on paper. It received money from Eli for years and quietly distributed it elsewhere.  
The photograph with someone cut out of it turns out to be from Birmingham in the 1960s. Eli and Leon stand outside a tiny law office. Between them, a young Black man has been removed from the frame. His hand remains visible at the edge. Callie keeps digging.  
Daniel and Sarah Caldwell arrive at the house. They are not theatrical. They are not greedy. They are not there to claim a father. They are uncomfortable, guarded, and angry at being summoned by a dead man into a white family's grief. Callie treats them like evidence. Daniel pushes back. Sarah produces old letters from Eli. Not love letters. Not paternal letters exactly. Something stranger: tuition checks, legal advice, birthday notes, offers of help never phrased as charity.  
Callie asks if Eli was their father. Sarah laughs once, bitterly: "No. That would have been cleaner."  
The room fractures. Ruth knew them. Not well, but enough. Leon knows them too. The siblings feel betrayed. Callie feels vindicated and confused at the same time. If Eli was not their father, why are they in the will? Why did he spend decades supporting them? Why did Ruth allow it? Why is Leon so afraid of the answer?  
Because Callie challenged the will, Leon is required to play Eli's conditional video. Eli appears onscreen, alive again in the most infuriating way possible. He is older but alert, dressed too carefully, seated at his desk like a man staging his own deposition.  
"If you're watching this," he says, "somebody finally called bullshit. Good."  
The family stares. Eli explains that the will was built to be challenged. If no one challenged it, they could keep the cleaner story. The familiar story. The one where Eli Mori was difficult, disappointing, maybe dishonest, but basically theirs. If someone did challenge it, Leon would be authorized to release the sealed materials.  
Eli does not explain everything. He never could. Even in death, he cannot say the thing directly. He talks around it. Makes jokes. Offers legal framing. Uses phrases like "historical obligation," "imperfect restitution," and "a debt improperly structured as inheritance." Callie wants to throw something at the screen. She realizes she has not beaten Eli. She has followed his instructions. But the video changes the mystery. This is not about secret children. It is about why Eli wanted them to think it was.  
Callie corners Leon. Leon finally gives her the first piece. "Your father and me?" he says. "A Jew and a Negro in Birmingham. That wasn't a partnership. That was a dare." Callie reacts to the word. Leon does not soften. "That was the polite word. On the door I couldn't walk through. In the court I couldn't sit in. In the paper when they remembered to capitalize it. I was a Negro lawyer in Birmingham, Alabama. Your father was a Jew with a law degree and a death wish. That is not trivia. That is the weather."  
He tells her about the firm. Eli was young then. Brilliant, vain, idealistic, hungry. The son of people who had taught him that survival and justice were not always the same skill. He wanted to matter. He wanted to be brave. He wanted history to look at him and nod. Leon was young too, but not naive. He understood Birmingham better than Eli ever could. He understood which smiles were threats. Which judges would let you speak but not win. Which courthouse clerks were feeding information to men with ropes in their trunks.  
They started the firm anyway. Their first real case was Caleb Caldwell. Caleb was a Black man married to a white woman. That alone made him a target. He was not an activist celebrity. Not a symbol. Not a speech. He was a man with a wife, newborn twins, a job, a temper, and the misfortune of becoming legally useful to two young lawyers who believed the law could be forced to behave.  
They started the firm anyway. Their first real case was **CALEB CALDWELL**. Caleb was a Black man married to a white woman. That alone made him a target. He was not an activist celebrity. Not a symbol. Not a speech. He was a man with a wife, newborn twins, a job, a temper, and the misfortune of becoming legally useful to two young lawyers who believed the law could be forced to behave.  
But Leon agreed. Because the case mattered. Because Caleb wanted to fight. Because Eli's strategy was clever. Because they were young enough to mistake cleverness for control.  
Eli devised the move. A filing. A threat of public testimony. A sealed affidavit that did not stay sealed. A legal pressure tactic meant to force powerful men into retreat. Instead, it painted a target on Caleb. Caleb was lynched. Not because Eli wanted it. Not because Leon wanted it. Because they misjudged the monster. They thought they were playing law. The city was playing terror.  
But the failure did not end there. In the devastating wake of the murder, Eli and Leon were confronted with the terrifying reality of their choices. There was active, deep complicity on the parts of Eli and Leon in not seeing the lynching of Caleb settled legally. They held the leverage to demand an explosive federal inquiry, but when Caleb's killers threatened to slaughter Caleb's surviving widow and newborn twins, Eli and Leon made a trade. They chose to stay silent to protect Caleb's widow, burying the murder evidence they had promised to bring to light, trading justice for physical survival.  
That was the moment Eli broke. Before Caleb, Eli was theatrical but sincere. Vain, yes. Hungry, yes. But alive with purpose. He believed words could protect bodies. He believed the right argument in the right room could bend the world toward justice. After Caleb, something in him curdled.  
He still became a respected civil rights attorney. In some ways, Caleb's death made him better. Sharper. More careful. More relentless. He won cases. He built a reputation. He became the man people called when the case was dangerous and impossible. But privately, he lost faith in clean truth. Truth had gotten Caleb killed.  
So Eli became a man of obfuscation. Half-truths. Legal fog. Emotional misdirection. He learned to bury danger inside paperwork and call it protection. He had to actively master legal fog to hide the very murder evidence he promised never to bring to court. He learned to joke when ashamed, stall when cornered, charm when accused, and make every confession so indirect that no one could use it against him or anyone he loved. He became the shifty old Eli Callie knew. Not because he had never cared. Because caring had once gotten someone killed.  
That does not excuse him. It explains the shape of the wreckage.  
After Caleb's death, Eli began sending money to Caleb's widow and children. At first through Leon. Then through shell charities. Then through trusts. Then directly, indirectly, always awkwardly, always with language that made generosity feel like liability management. The vintage fountain pen left behind wasn't just a writing tool; it was the instrument Eli used to execute the quiet, off-the-books financial transfers that guaranteed the widow's silence and survival.  
Ruth found out early. Callie assumes Ruth tolerated betrayal. Ruth corrects her. "I was not sharing my husband with another woman," Ruth says. "I was sharing him with the first ghost he ever brought home."  
Ruth knew Eli was broken. She knew he was paying a debt that could never be paid. She watched him become less emotionally available to his own children because some part of him remained forever in a Birmingham street with Caleb Caldwell's body. She hated him for it. She pitied him for it. She enabled it because stopping him would have been obscene.  
"You think he gave those children too much?" Ruth says. "Callie, he helped take their father. There was no too much."  
That is what Callie cannot metabolize. She wanted Eli's sin to be simple. An affair. A secret family. A fraud. A deadbeat father who gave love somewhere else because he was selfish. The truth is worse. Eli's absence in her life was not caused by another romance. It was caused by guilt. His emotional vagueness was not merely cowardice. It was scar tissue. His legal games were not just opportunism. They were the survival strategy of a man who never again trusted direct action after the first direct action got a client killed.  
Mori and Ruth had forced the law on her because of this terrifying secret. They wanted a sharp, razor-focused lawyer in the family as a generational contingency plan—someone who could act as a defensive shield if the past ever broke through the perimeter and threatened criminal exposure for withholding evidence in a capital murder case.  
Callie is furious because the truth does not absolve Eli, but it makes hating him harder. The released estate documents reveal the architecture of Eli's lifelong penance. The fake charity. The trusts. The letters. The checks. The school payments. The quiet repairs after emergencies. The birthday gifts sent late or anonymously. The legal favors extended to Caleb's descendants and never explained.  
Daniel and Sarah are not Eli's secret children. They are Caleb's children. Eli did not father them. He stood in the hole where their father should have been and called that standing "responsibility."  
Daniel rejects that framing. He sees the money as blood accounting. Rent paid on an absence. Eli did not raise them. Eli reimbursed them. Sarah is less certain. Eli was clumsy, evasive, sometimes insulting in his attempts to help. But he showed up more than many innocent men did. She resents him. She also misses him. She hates that both can be true.  
Leon carries his own guilt. Callie tries to put the whole thing on Eli. Leon refuses the gift. "Do not make him larger than he was," Leon says. "I was there. I saw the board. I moved the piece too." Leon has spent decades allowing Eli to be the face of the sin because Eli wanted the burden and Leon let him carry it. That was Leon's cowardice. Eli became the guilty legend. Leon became the dignified survivor. Caleb became the ghost between them.  
The family collapses under the weight of the truth. At the mandatory post-shiva dinner Eli required, nobody wants the inheritance anymore. Callie's siblings feel robbed retroactively. Their father's distance now has an explanation, but explanation is not restitution. Ruth is accused of choosing Eli's guilt over her children's clarity. Daniel refuses to be part of a dead white lawyer's redemption mechanism. Sarah accuses Daniel of pretending the money never mattered. Leon admits he almost destroyed Eli's final instructions because he could not bear to watch another generation be recruited into Caleb's death.  
Callie finally says what she has been circling since the beginning: "So this was his last con. Make us think he had a secret family so we would be relieved when it was only a lynching." The room goes silent. It is cruel. It is also not entirely wrong. Ruth slaps her. Not because Callie lied. Because she reduced the dead again.  
The dinner ends in ruins. Eli's will, designed to force truth, has produced only accusation.  
That night, Callie sits alone in Eli's office. She opens the box he left for her, hidden behind a false back in an old file cabinet—a hiding place he once showed her when she was a child and then pretended not to remember. The box is labeled:  
**CALLIE \- IF YOU GET THIS FAR**  
Inside are unsent letters. Draft apologies. Notes on arguments they had. Birthday cards he wrote and never mailed. A list titled:  
**THINGS YOU WILL ASSUME I DID FOR SELFISH REASONS**  
It is funny until it is devastating. Then Callie finds the oldest item in the box. A photograph of young Eli, young Leon, and Caleb Caldwell standing outside the firm's first office. In this version, Caleb has not been cut out. Eli is smiling too hard. Leon looks cautious. Caleb looks amused, skeptical, alive.  
Behind the photograph is a note from Eli. Not a legal memo. Not a joke. Not a performance. Just one sentence:  
*He trusted me.*  
That is the closest Eli ever came to telling the truth plainly. Callie breaks. Not because Eli is redeemed. Because she finally sees the origin point. Everything she hated about him—the evasions, the jokes, the manipulations, the emotional fog, the legal games, the fake awards, the strange gifts, the inability to say the thing he meant—all of it grew around this wound. Eli did not become a shyster because he lacked a conscience. He became one because his conscience never stopped screaming and he built a whole personality to drown it out.  
The next morning, Callie stops trying to execute Eli's will. She gathers Ruth, Leon, Daniel, Sarah, and her siblings. She tells them Eli does not get to control the living just because he staged his confession well. His plan was manipulative. Cruel, even. It forced people into pain he was too dead to answer for. It made Daniel and Sarah relive their father's absence. It made Ruth defend a marriage no one understood. It made Leon carry guilt into one more room. But burning the will would also protect Eli's favorite lie: that confusion is safer than truth.  
So Callie proposes something else. They will settle the estate, but not as Eli wrote it. Daniel and Sarah will receive what Eli intended, not as charity and not as hush money, but as an acknowledged debt. If they want it public, the family will support that. If they want privacy, the family will honor that. Ruth will be protected, but not turned into Eli's moral shield. Leon will release the relevant records. Caleb's name will be restored to the family story, the firm history, and the public record Eli spent decades bending around him.  
Eli's children may accept or reject the objects he left them. No one has to pretend an object is love. No one has to pretend money is forgiveness. Callie says the will was not a map. It was a symptom. That is the difference. Eli wanted the family to solve him. Callie refuses. She chooses to tell the truth about him instead.  
They hold a second informal reading. Not the legal will. The real inheritance. Objects are placed on the table. The watch. The pen. The case file. The photograph. The fake award. The broken chess clock. The menorah. Each object is stripped of Eli's intended theatrical meaning and given back to the living.  
Daniel refuses Eli's personal letter but accepts copies of the Caleb file. Sarah accepts the letter and the money, though she says she may hate both forever. Leon returns an old sign from the first firm: *MORI & BARNES, ATTORNEYS AT LAW*. Callie notices the order of names. Mori first. Leon sees her notice. "Your father insisted," he says. "Said the city would burn the place faster if my name came first." "Was he right?" Leon smiles sadly. "That was the problem with your father. Sometimes the cowardice and the strategy wore the same suit."  
Ruth gives Callie the fake award. Callie almost refuses it. Then she sees the back. A smaller engraving, hidden under felt, reads:  
**FOR CALEB \- WHO DESERVED THE HONOR AND GOT THE BILL**  
Callie realizes the award was not simply vanity. It was also self-punishment. A private, ugly little monument to the honor Eli knew he could never honestly accept.  
The final legal papers are pulled forward to cement the historic settlement. Sitting at the table across from Leon and the twins, Callie takes her father's vintage 1965 fountain pen to execute the document. She signs her name on the line: CALLIE MORI.  
As the ink settles, her hand freezes. Her eyes track across the page, visually catching the alignment of her signature directly parallel to the beneficiary lines. In total silence, never spoken aloud, she sees the precise structural and phonetic symmetry between her own name and the dead client's: CALLIE and CALEB. Eli tends to call them both Cal, something we see in his interactions with Caleb, but don't see in his interactions with Callie. The exact spatial footprint matches perfectly. The floor drops out from under her as she realizes her forced career was never about parental pride; she was trained in the law from birth to be the specific tool required to manage her father's silent complicity. She is choosing a protective, human peace over a clean, destructive legal verdict—making the exact same trade her father made sixty years ago.  
But the final legal maneuver belongs neither to the courtroom nor to the ghost of Caleb. After the mystery box is solved, Leon quietly steps forward and produces an alternative will written entirely by Eli. It is an instrument left in Leon's custody, representing Eli's best outcome effort and a poignant nod to his ultimate belief in his daughter's ability to ferret out the truth and do the right thing.  
Callie reviews the document. By choosing to file this alternative will, Callie effectively renders herself her father's daughter, taking the path of moral clarity by way of ethical flexibility. She uses the law not as a rigid sword of judgment, but as a malleable shield to protect the living and formalize the peace they have just constructed.  
At a small memorial gathering, Callie gives the eulogy she refused to give earlier. She does not redeem Eli. She does not perform forgiveness. She tells the truth.  
Eli Mori was once brave. Then he was broken. He helped build a firm in a city that hated what it represented. He took a first case that mattered. He made a choice he believed would protect a man named Caleb Caldwell. That choice helped get Caleb killed. Eli spent the rest of his life trying to pay a debt no money could settle. He became a better lawyer because of Caleb. He became a worse man because of Caleb. He helped people. He deceived people. He loved badly. He hid constantly. He confused guilt with duty and duty with affection. He gave generously and made generosity hard to receive. He turned truth into paperwork because the first plain truth he ever told got someone killed.  
Callie says: "My father spent his life paying a debt he could never settle. That does not make him innocent. It makes him ours."  
Ruth listens. Daniel and Sarah stand near the back. Leon cries silently, with the exhaustion of a man who has finally heard Caleb's name spoken in the room where Eli's legend was supposed to be buried.  
Later, Callie packs up Eli's office. She places the fake award on a shelf, misspelling facing outward. Then she tapes Caleb's photograph beside it. Not Eli alone. Not the fake honor alone. The wound and the costume. The truth and the fraud.  
Before leaving, Callie finds one last envelope under the award. Of course. Inside is a note from Eli.  
*Too much?*  
Behind it, a second scrap:  
*I know.*  
Callie stares at the notes. For the first time, Eli's joke does not erase the pain. It admits it. She folds the notes and keeps them.  
The final image is the shelf: the fake award, the photograph of Eli, Leon, and Caleb, and the empty space around them. A fraud. A confession. A debt. A life broken by guilt. An inheritance too complicated to refuse.

Callie’s last words to Ruth.

*He trusted me.*

**End.**

# **NOTES**
-The initial premise was the word play in the title. There is a similar Latin term which means 'Remember, You too will die'. The other side of the word play was Mori leaving behind various items as mementos of life as lessons to his daughter, who unbeknownst to her is on the same path to cynicism and self-loathing.
-There are two distinct timelines.
